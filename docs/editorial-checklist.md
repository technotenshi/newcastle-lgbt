# Guidelines for Writing Advocacy-Forward LGBTQ+ History and News

Apply this checklist to any news article that covers sensitive historical, political, or advocacy-forward LGBTQ+ subject matter (movement history, government policy, corporate accountability, discrimination, etc.). It was written after a full editorial pass on [`content/news/20260601-01-true-history-of-pride-erasure.md`](../content/news/20260601-01-true-history-of-pride-erasure.md) surfaced the patterns below across multiple drafts. Use it alongside [`docs/content-authoring.md`](content-authoring.md).

## Editorial stance: this is an advocacy site, not neutral journalism

This site exists to advocate for the Newcastle, WA LGBTQ+ community. When a policy, initiative, bill, or public figure works against that community's interests, articles should say so plainly and support the conclusion with evidence, not present it as one equally weighted "side" of a debate. Treating a documented harm and a campaign's denial of that harm as symmetrical is a failure of this checklist, not neutrality.

This does not license inaccuracy, it raises the bar for it. To be usefully critical:

- **Verify before characterizing.** Read the actual bill, initiative, or policy text before describing its effect, and quote it directly where possible. A critique built on a claim that doesn't survive contact with the source text hands opponents an easy way to dismiss the whole article.
- **Fact-check supporters with the same rigor as opponents.** If a policy's supporters make a specific, checkable claim (a statistic, a legal argument, a cited authority, a named expert), verify it. Report when it doesn't hold up and show the evidence, rather than only relaying the opposing campaign's rebuttal.
- **Be precise about genuinely disputed mechanics.** When a specific detail is legitimately contested (for example, whether a bill's text requires a particular procedure or merely permits it), say so accurately rather than choosing whichever reading is more dramatic. Precision on one contested detail does not require softening a pattern of harm that is otherwise well documented elsewhere in the piece.
- **Reach a conclusion.** An article should end with a synthesized, evidence-backed assessment of what the policy does and who it harms, not stop after listing "arguments for" and "arguments against" as if the piece had no point of view.
- **Name the people responsible, not just the institution, when the evidence supports it.** This site is not afraid to name a donor, legislator, spokesperson, executive, or candidate by name when a source ties them directly to the statement, vote, donation, or decision in question. Don't retreat to "a spokesperson said" or "the campaign claimed" when the record names who actually said or did it. The evidentiary bar does not change: a quote needs a source, a donation needs a filing, a vote needs a roll call. Naming a specific person is not itself the risk; naming one without a credible source is.

Apply this stance together with, not instead of, the precision rules in items 1 through 20 below: being critical of a policy and the people who advance it, by name, is fully compatible with never overstating what a bill's text actually requires, never fabricating a quote or statistic, and never assigning blame to an entire identity group instead of the specific organization, official, or donor responsible.

## 1. Center marginalized people as full human beings

Do not define LGBTQ+ people primarily through homelessness, poverty, sex work, incarceration, trauma or confrontation.

Describe the conditions they faced, but also preserve their agency, relationships, political goals, organizing work and futures.

Avoid phrases such as:

* "They had nothing left to lose."
* "The world had discarded them."
* "They fought because they had already lost everything."

Prefer language such as:

* "They faced sustained police harassment and exclusion from public institutions."
* "They resisted despite the risks to their safety, housing and livelihoods."
* "They built networks of care where existing institutions had failed them."

## 2. Do not romanticize violence or deprivation

Resistance should not be measured only by who threw the first object, fought hardest or confronted police most aggressively.

Use broader descriptions that recognize organizing, mutual aid, testimony, protest and community defense.

Prefer:

* "Resisted the raid"
* "Sustained the uprising"
* "Organized in its aftermath"
* "Built institutions after Stonewall"

Avoid turning suffering into dramatic scenery or presenting poverty as proof of political authenticity.

## 3. Distinguish overlapping identities and experiences

Do not treat transgender people, drag performers, sex workers, unhoused youth and gender-nonconforming people as interchangeable categories.

These identities and experiences sometimes overlapped, but they were not synonymous.

Use precise language such as:

> "Transgender people, drag performers and other gender-nonconforming participants"

Do not assign modern identity labels to historical figures unless those labels are supported by their own words, reputable biographies or careful historical scholarship.

## 4. Avoid replacing one historical myth with another

Correcting the erasure of transgender people and people of color should not require creating new unsupported founder stories.

Avoid absolute claims about:

* Who started Stonewall
* Who threw the first brick or punch
* Which group single-handedly created Pride
* Whether one person or identity group stood at the center of every stage of the movement

When accounts conflict, say so directly.

Useful language includes:

* "Accounts differ."
* "Her role in the opening moments remains disputed."
* "Several witnesses later attributed..."
* "The surviving record does not establish..."

Historical uncertainty does not weaken the article. Acknowledging it strengthens credibility.

## 5. Attribute hostile and coded language

Right-wing political terms should never appear as neutral descriptions.

Put phrases such as "gender ideology," "biological truth," "cross-dressing" and "gender-appropriate clothing" in quotation marks and identify who used them.

Explain the function of the language when relevant. For example:

> The order used the political phrase "gender ideology" to characterize transgender identity and gender-inclusive policy as an extremist doctrine.

Do not repeat coded language in headlines, captions or promotional copy without context.

## 6. Contextualize outdated historical terminology

Retain outdated words when they are part of an organization's official historical name, a direct quotation or an archival record.

Add a short explanation that the terminology reflected the period and is not appropriate as a general modern label.

Do not silently modernize the names of historical organizations, but do not adopt their terminology as the article's voice.

## 7. Name institutions and decision-makers

Avoid blaming "the movement," "the community," "gay people," "lesbians" or "white gay men" as undifferentiated groups.

Identify the organization, organizer, leadership body, event committee, government agency or political tendency responsible whenever the record permits, and do not stop at the organization's name when a specific person within it is the one on record. When a filing lists a donor, a quote is attributed to a named spokesperson, or a vote is recorded under a specific legislator's name, use that name. This applies to current political actors as much as historical ones: a hedge fund manager bankrolling an initiative, a legislator explaining a vote, a candidate's undisclosed affiliation, all belong in the article by name, sourced the same way any other claim is.

Instead of:

> "The movement silenced Rivera."

Write:

> "Rivera encountered exclusion from influential gay and lesbian organizations, including efforts by some rally organizers to prevent her from speaking."

Specific attribution produces stronger accountability and reduces unfair collective blame.

## 8. Avoid identity-based stereotypes

Words such as "loud," "aggressive," "outlandish," "militant" and "unignorable" can carry racialized, gendered or anti-trans connotations.

Use concrete descriptions of conduct instead.

Instead of:

> "She was one of the loudest voices."

Write:

> "She became one of the most persistent and visible advocates."

Instead of:

> "Militancy returned to the streets."

Write:

> "Direct action and confrontational protest returned to the streets."

## 9. Explain systems, not just personal hardship

Connect individual experiences to the policies and institutions producing them.

Relevant systems may include:

* Discriminatory liquor licensing
* Police raids and invasive searches
* Housing exclusion
* Family rejection
* Criminalization of sex work
* Racism within LGBTQ+ organizations
* Trans exclusion from legislation
* Federal restrictions on health information
* Corporate political contributions

This approach prevents the article from presenting oppression as a series of isolated personal tragedies.

## 10. Be precise about police conduct

Do not adopt the language of police compliance as the article's default frame.

Instead of:

> "The patrons did not comply."

Consider:

> "Unlike during many previous raids, the patrons did not leave quietly."

Describe invasive searches, detention practices and clothing enforcement plainly. Do not present discriminatory police classifications as legitimate or medically neutral.

## 11. Do not assume motives when actions can be documented

Political donation records can demonstrate contradiction. They do not automatically prove what executives privately believed.

Instead of:

> "The company's Pride support was cynical."

Write:

> "The company's Pride campaign conflicted with political contributions made through its PAC."

Similarly, avoid terms such as "quietly," "deliberately" or "masquerading as" unless reporting establishes secrecy or intent.

Focus first on what an institution did, who was affected and what evidence supports the conclusion.

## 12. Limit absolute claims

Words such as "every," "first," "only," "all," "entirely" and "never" require unusually strong evidence.

Claims that an organization was the first shelter, the model for every later program or the only organization of its kind should be independently verified.

When the evidence is less certain, use:

* "Among the earliest"
* "Helped influence later programs"
* "One of the first documented"
* "Became an important model"

## 13. Preserve material context without sensationalizing it

Details about organized crime, sex work, panhandling, weapons, imprisonment and street survival may be historically relevant. Include them when they explain how institutions operated or how people survived.

Do not repeat them for atmosphere, shock or moral contrast.

For example, explain that organized crime controlled many gay bars partly because discriminatory licensing practices excluded openly gay establishments from legitimate business systems.

## 14. Avoid the myth of universal gay affluence

When discussing corporate Pride, do not imply that LGBTQ+ people broadly possess high disposable incomes.

Explain that advertisers often targeted a narrow image of the affluent, usually white, gay or lesbian consumer while overlooking poverty, housing insecurity and economic inequality within the larger community.

## 15. Describe government erasure concretely

"Erasure" is a powerful and appropriate analytical term, but support it with specific actions.

Identify what was:

* Deleted
* Renamed
* Taken offline
* Defunded
* Restricted
* Reclassified
* Excluded from official policy
* Restored under court order

Distinguish between removing references to transgender people, denying federal recognition of transgender identities and excluding transgender people from programs or institutions. These actions are related, but they are not identical.

## 16. Treat quotations as evidence, not decoration

When using charged quotations, identify the speaker, date, setting and source.

Do not rely on a quotation to establish a broad historical claim without explaining how it fits the larger record.

Verify famous lines, especially those that have become part of movement mythology.

## 17. Maintain proportionality in the conclusion

The ending should synthesize the reporting, not introduce broader claims than the article has established.

Prefer formulations such as:

> "Transgender people, drag performers, unhoused queer youth and sex workers participated in the uprising and helped build organizations in its aftermath."

Avoid claims implying that one group alone created the uprising, the movement or Pride.

A forceful conclusion can remain precise.

## 18. Use an accountability-forward structure

For long historical explainers, organize the story around a clear chain:

1. What happened
2. Who participated
3. What institutions were built afterward
4. Who was excluded
5. How that exclusion operated
6. How the same pattern appears in current policy
7. What evidence supports the comparison

This keeps advocacy grounded in documented actions rather than rhetoric alone.

## 19. Separate fact, interpretation and moral judgment

Readers should be able to tell which statements are:

* Documented facts
* Disputed historical accounts
* Interpretations supported by evidence
* Moral or political conclusions

Use attribution and transitions to make those distinctions visible.

For example:

> "Donation records show a conflict between the company's Pride sponsorship and its PAC contributions."

Then:

> "That conflict raises questions about whether corporate branding provides meaningful support to the communities most affected by anti-LGBTQ+ policy."

## 20. Apply a final language check

Before publication, review every paragraph for these questions:

* Does this sentence reduce a person to trauma?
* Does it confuse transgender identity with drag?
* Does it assign blame to an entire identity group?
* Does it adopt police or right-wing terminology?
* Does it claim more than the evidence proves?
* Does it romanticize poverty, violence or confrontation?
* Does it conceal the responsible institution through passive voice?
* Does it describe marginalized people as agents, organizers and community members?
* Is every charged claim attributed and sourced?
* Could hostile readers reasonably distort this wording because it is vague or overstated?

The goal is not to soften the article's politics. The goal is to make its criticism more precise, defensible and difficult to dismiss.
