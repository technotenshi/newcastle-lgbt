:Dateline Washington's two entries in Erin Reed's September 8, 2026 national guide to congressional Democrats with anti-trans voting records are U.S. Reps. Marie Gluesenkamp Perez (D-WA-03) and Kim Schrier (D-WA-08).[^1] Reed's guide credits Schrier with a single named vote, a service-academy sports amendment to the defense bill, and describes Perez more broadly as having "taken multiple anti-trans votes," naming one specifically, a bill it describes as defunding public schools that support transgender students, and separately criticizing Perez's CNN remarks about "nuance" in the sports debate.[^1] The guide's Washington entry does not itself name the sports amendment for Perez, but as this article details below, Reed's own reporting on that amendment and the House Clerk's roll call record both show she voted for it too. On the two nationwide sports-ban bills Congress has considered since 2023, meanwhile, Perez and Schrier both voted no every time.[^2][^3]

## The Vote Both Cast

On September 10, 2025, during floor debate on the fiscal year 2026 National Defense Authorization Act (H.R. 3838), Rep. Nancy Mace (R-SC) offered Part A Amendment No. 15, barring superintendents of the U.S. service academies (West Point, Annapolis, the Air Force and Coast Guard academies, and the Merchant Marine Academy) from allowing a cadet or midshipman the amendment defines as male to compete on a women's athletic team. The House Clerk's roll call record shows it passed 227 to 201. Perez and Schrier were two of just 10 House Democrats who voted Aye, joining 217 Republicans.[^4]

Mace herself described the amendment in stark, gendered terms rather than as a technical eligibility rule. "This amendment ends the nonsense, protects fairness, and ensures female athletes at our nation's military academies are not forced to give up opportunities they've worked hard to earn," she said in a press release the day before the vote, framing the cadets and midshipmen the amendment excludes as men rather than as the transgender women they are.[^5] That framing, not a neutral description, is Mace's own; the amendment's practical effect is to bar transgender women training to become U.S. military officers from competing on their academy's women's teams.

The athletics amendment was one of six anti-trans provisions Republicans attached to the NDAA that week, alongside amendments restricting TRICARE and Exceptional Family Member Program coverage of gender-affirming care, removing gender identity from military demographic surveys, limiting access to single-sex facilities, and banning Pride flags at Defense Department workplaces.[^6] Perez and Schrier's support did not extend to the rest of that package: both voted No on the amendment removing gender identity from military surveys,[^7] and Schrier voted No on the facility-access amendment as well; Perez does not appear in that vote's record at all, meaning she was absent rather than opposed.[^8]

## What Exclusion Costs

The exclusion this amendment and the facility restriction described above impose is not abstract. A 2019 peer-reviewed study in *Pediatrics*, drawing on a national survey of 3,673 transgender and nonbinary adolescents, found that school policies requiring restroom and locker-room use based on sex assigned at birth were associated with more than double the rate of sexual assault among transgender girls specifically: a risk ratio of 2.49 compared with schools without such a restriction.[^9] That is the same category of policy Perez and Schrier's colleagues attached to this bill, and the same reasoning, that a transgender person's exclusion from a facility or a team is a manageable trade-off, that the amendment both representatives voted for extends to athletics.

The evidence on exclusion from ordinary school life points the same direction. The Trevor Project's 2025 national survey of more than 16,000 LGBTQ+ young people ages 13 to 24 found that transgender and nonbinary youth who had access to gender-neutral bathrooms at school, clothing that matched their gender, and pronouns respected by the people they live with reported meaningfully lower rates of attempting suicide than those who did not.[^10] Barring transgender cadets and midshipmen from women's athletics at the service academies affects a small number of people directly, but it sits inside a much larger, well-documented pattern: institutions that treat a transgender person's exclusion from ordinary participation as a reasonable compromise are institutions the evidence says put that person at measurably greater risk.

The same day, the House passed the full NDAA 231 to 196. Perez voted for the bill; Schrier voted against it, one of 192 Democratic no votes on a defense authorization bill that Republicans supported almost unanimously.[^11]

## Where the Record Splits

Reed's guide does not mention it, but the roll call record shows the September 2025 sports amendment is a departure from how both women had voted before. In April 2023, when the Republican-controlled House first took up a nationwide ban on transgender girls and women in school and college sports under Title IX (H.R. 734), the vote split almost perfectly along party lines: 219 Republicans in favor, 203 Democrats against, including Perez and Schrier.[^2] When the House took up a nearly identical successor bill in January 2025 (H.R. 28), the outcome was the same: 218 to 206, with Perez and Schrier again voting no.[^3] Both bills would have applied nationwide, to K-12 schools and colleges receiving federal funding, not just to the five service academies the September 2025 amendment covers.

Eight months after that amendment vote, the two representatives' paths diverged further. On May 20, 2026, the House passed H.R. 2616, the Stopping Indoctrination and Protecting Kids Act, 217 to 198. As a condition of federal funding, the bill's text requires public elementary and middle schools to obtain parental consent before changing a covered student's gender markers, pronouns, or preferred name on any school form, or their sex-based accommodations, including locker-room or bathroom access. It separately bars the use of those federal education funds "to teach or advance concepts related to gender ideology," defining that term by cross-reference to Executive Order 14168, itself titled around "defending women from gender ideology extremism."[^12] The House Clerk's roll call shows Perez voted for it, one of only eight House Democrats to do so; Schrier voted against it.[^13][^14]

Perez explained her vote in her own statement two days later, framing it around parental involvement rather than restriction: "A child's name and sense of self are not small things. They are fundamental, and on something that important I don't believe parents should be cut out of the loop. This bill requires schools to get a parent's consent before changing an elementary or middle school student's pronouns or name on school forms. My heart breaks for children who don't feel safe letting their parents into their world. But divorcing a young child's school life from their home life doesn't resolve the underlying problem of an unsupportive family. It can leave that child more alone. I voted for this bill because I believe the answer is to bring families in."[^15] Rep. Mark Takano (D-CA), chair of the Congressional Equality Caucus, rejects that framing and calls the bill a forced-outing measure regardless of a parent's intentions: "Republicans claim to be the party of small government, but they have no problem bringing the full force of the federal government down against children."[^16] Takano's objection and Perez's own statement describe the same mechanism, a parent learning a child's transgender status through the school, differently: Perez frames it as reuniting a child's school and home life, Takano as removing a school's ability to judge, case by case, whether that disclosure is safe.

## The Cost of Forced Disclosure

The mechanism at the center of H.R. 2616, a school required to tell parents about a change to a transgender student's name, pronouns, or facility access regardless of whether home is safe, has its own body of evidence, and it does not support Perez's framing. A landmark 2009 study in *Pediatrics* that followed 224 LGB young adults found that those who experienced high levels of rejection from their families as adolescents were 8.4 times more likely to have attempted suicide, and 5.9 times more likely to report high levels of depression, than those whose families did not reject them.[^17] The Trevor Project's 2025 survey found the same pattern holds for transgender and nonbinary youth specifically: those living in very accepting communities attempted suicide at less than a third the rate of those in very unaccepting ones.[^10]

Perez's own statement acknowledges this risk directly. "My heart breaks for children who don't feel safe letting their parents into their world," she said, before concluding that reuniting a child's school and home life is still the better answer. The research above describes exactly the children she names, the ones for whom disclosure is not safe, and it points the opposite direction from her conclusion: family rejection, not a school's discretion to withhold a disclosure it judges unsafe, is the documented harm. A bill that removes that discretion does not protect the "unsupportive family" Perez describes worrying about. It removes the one buffer a transgender student in that situation has.

## "We Miss Some of the Nuance"

Reed's guide also cites Perez for criticizing how Democrats talk about trans-inclusive sports policy. In a July 2026 CNN interview with anchor Pamela Brown, weeks after the U.S. Supreme Court upheld state bans on transgender athletes in girls' sports, Perez described town halls where constituents who had spent years driving daughters to practice, hoping for an athletic scholarship, pressed her on the issue. "When we rush to moralize and be like, 'This is all about love vs. hate,' I think we miss some of the nuance," she said, arguing that decisions about sports eligibility are better made locally than by federal mandate.[^18]

The Supreme Court ruling Perez was responding to, issued June 30, 2026 in West Virginia v. B.P.J. and a companion Idaho case, upheld both states' laws barring transgender girls and women from female school sports teams: unanimously on Title IX grounds, and 6-3 on the constitutional Equal Protection claim.[^19] The ruling gives states latitude to ban transgender athletes from women's sports. It does not require any state, including Washington, to do so.

## Washington's Own Policy Is Untouched, For Now

State Superintendent Chris Reykdal responded to the ruling the same day it came down: "Today's decision reinforces Washington state's ability to adopt and implement our own laws in alignment with our values. Our Legislature has been clear: All students are welcome here."[^20] Attorney General Nick Brown called the outcome "dismaying" but was direct about its limits in Washington: "This ruling does not change Washington law, and we will continue to uphold the rights and dignity of trans youth."[^21] The Washington Interscholastic Activities Association has allowed students to compete according to gender identity since 2007, out of roughly 250,000 student-athletes statewide of whom five to ten are transgender, the same population this site examined in its coverage of the state's November 2026 sports-eligibility ballot initiative.[^21] (*See our [prior coverage](/news/2026/09/04/anti-lgbtq-ballot-initiatives-documented-risks) of that measure and the medical evidence around gender-affirming care generally, covered in [our reporting on the federal Medicaid rule](/news/2026/09/06/federal-assault-transgender-youth-healthcare).*)

That state-level backstop is exactly why the federal votes matter on their own terms: Congress does not set Washington's high school or college sports policy, but it does set eligibility rules for the five federal service academies directly, and it sets conditions on federal school funding that reach every public school district in the state, including Newcastle's.

## What Washington Voters Deserve to Know

Perez and Schrier share exactly one vote that put them on Reed's list: barring transgender women from women's sports at the nation's service academies, a concrete, documented step that shuts specific cadets and midshipmen out of specific teams, with a documented safety cost behind it. On every other trans-rights vote in this record, including the two much broader nationwide sports bans and every other anti-trans amendment attached to the same NDAA that week, they voted the same way, against restriction. That makes the September 2025 amendment worth real scrutiny rather than a footnote: both representatives are willing to support a categorical exclusion when it is scoped narrowly enough, even inside a single bill's amendment package.

Beyond that shared vote, the two records diverge, but neither earns a pass. Perez's May 2026 vote for H.R. 2616 and her CNN remarks rest on a belief she has stated in her own words: that keeping parents informed protects children, even when a child's home life is not supportive. It is a genuine, stated conviction. It is also, on the evidence above, backward: family rejection is the documented harm, not the safeguard, for exactly the children she says she wants to reach. A representative who votes to exclude transgender cadets from academy sports, and to strip schools of discretion over whether disclosing a student's transgender status to their family is safe, has cast two votes with a measurable cost to the people affected by them, regardless of the reasoning behind either one. Schrier's record is narrower, one categorical exclusion vote inside an otherwise consistent record against these bills, but that distinction doesn't erase the cost of the vote she did cast.

This site is not going to tell Washington voters how to vote in a race where the only alternative on the November ballot is a Republican who would likely be worse on every question examined here. But "better than the alternative" is not the same as "good," and accountability for a sitting representative does not end at the ballot box. Perez and Schrier represent Washington's transgender constituents too, and a record like this one, an exclusion vote both cast and, for Perez, a forced-disclosure bill she advanced on top of it, is not something a Washington congressional delegation should get to treat as a routine trade-off. It should cost them constituent pressure now, and it should cost them a serious primary challenge, from someone who won't cast either vote, next time.

## The Alternative

Washington voters already got a preview of what that challenge looks like. In WA-03, progressive Democrat Brent Hennrich ran to Perez's left in the August 2026 primary and finished third with 16.8% of the vote, behind Perez's 36.6% and Republican John Braun's 39.6%.[^22] Hennrich's platform commits to supporting "LGBTQ+ anti-discrimination legislation and any other anti-discrimination language at the Federal level,"[^23] and Progressive Victory, a national group that tracks and backs progressive candidates, lists him among the ones it says it "loves."[^24] After the primary, Hennrich declined to endorse Perez and put the burden back on her directly: "I believe the onus is on MGP to earn every vote. She needs to reach out to those who feel harmed by her voting record and work with them to find a path to earning their support."[^22] His campaign didn't lay out a detailed platform on the specific votes examined in this article the way this article was able to verify for Perez's actual record, so this isn't an unqualified endorsement of every position he holds. But he is the clearest example Washington has right now of what running against this record, rather than around it, looks like.

WA-08 didn't have a comparable option this cycle. Democrat Keith Arnold challenged Schrier in the primary and drew a small share of the vote, well behind both Schrier and the second-place Republican. Arnold's own voter guide profile shows why he can't be named as an alternative the way Hennrich can: asked directly whether he supported transgender people's participation in sports consistent with their gender identity, and asked about marriage equality, he answered neither question.[^25] That gap is itself worth naming: the district with the narrower of these two vote records has no documented alternative waiting for 2028. Building one starts with the constituent pressure described below, not at the next filing deadline.
