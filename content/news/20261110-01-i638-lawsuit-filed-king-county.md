:Dateline Parents of transgender and cisgender girls, TransFamilies and the Washington Education Association sued the State of Washington on Monday, November 9, in King County Superior Court to stop Initiative 638 from taking effect on **December 3**.[^1][^2] I-638 bars trans girls from girls' school sports, and it requires every student who competes in girls-only school athletics to get a health care provider to verify her sex first.[^3] This site [opposed I-638](/news/2026/10/28/washington-election-transgender-rights) and supports the families and educators challenging it.

## The lawsuit makes four claims under the state constitution

The plaintiffs ask the court to declare that I-638 violates the Washington Constitution and to bar the state from implementing or enforcing it.[^1] The complaint rests on four provisions:

- **Privacy (article I, section 7).** The constitution says "No person shall be disturbed in his private affairs, or his home invaded, without authority of law."[^4] The complaint says a student's body, genes and hormone levels "are as private as anything can be" and that I-638 makes disclosing them "the price of playing school sports."[^1]
- **Equal rights (article XXXI, section 1).** "Equality of rights and responsibility under the law shall not be denied or abridged on account of sex."[^4] The initiative's verification requirement applies to a student who competes in girls-only activities, and the text imposes nothing comparable on boys' teams.[^3] The complaint says that burden falls on girls "solely on account of sex."[^1]
- **Education (article IX, section 1).** The state's paramount duty is to educate all children "without distinction or preference on account of race, color, caste, or sex."[^4] The complaint says school sports are part of that education and that I-638 conditions girls' access to them on a requirement boys never face.[^1]
- **Improper amendment (article II, section 37).** "No act shall ever be revised or amended by mere reference to its title, but the act revised or the section amended shall be set forth at full length."[^4] I-638 amends RCW 28A.600.200 and 28A.640.020 and sets both out.[^3] The complaint says it also changes the Washington Law Against Discrimination, the Equal Educational Opportunity Law, RCW 28A.640.010 and the parents' rights statute, RCW 28A.605.005(2)(g), without printing them, and that voters were unaware of its effect on existing law.[^1]

The plaintiffs are parents of transgender and cisgender girls who play on Seattle public school teams, who say they will ask to proceed under pseudonyms to protect their children; TransFamilies, a Seattle-founded nonprofit that has supported transgender children and their families since 2008; and the Washington Education Association (WEA), whose members include the coaches, athletic directors, school nurses and counselors who would have to carry out the exclusion and verification rules.[^1] The ACLU of Washington (attorneys Adrien Leavitt, Tara Urs and La Rond Baker) and Legal Voice (managing director Julia Marks) represent TransFamilies and the parents, and Pacifica Law Group (attorneys Kai A. Smith and Paul J. Lawrence, among others) represents WEA.[^1][^5] The State of Washington is the only defendant.[^1]

The Standard and the Guardian describe the suit as also alleging a violation of anti-discrimination law.[^2][^6] The complaint's prayer for relief asks the court to declare violations of the four constitutional provisions above and no others.[^1] The anti-discrimination argument enters through the article II, section 37 claim, and through the complaint's statement that I-638 "requires schools to discriminate against transgender girls by excluding them from girls' sports."[^1]

The plaintiffs seek preliminary and permanent injunctions, and the ACLU of Washington says they will seek emergency relief to keep the law from taking effect on December 3.[^1][^5] As of November 10, none of the sources we read reports a hearing date or a ruling.

"Sports give young people a place to make friends and be part of a team, but Initiative 638 seeks to deny transgender girls those opportunities," said ACLU of Washington staff attorney **Adrien Leavitt**.[^5] WEA president **Janie White** said: "This new requirement is in direct conflict with the state's paramount duty to educate our children without discrimination."[^5] Legal Voice's **Julia Marks** said I-638 "creates an invasive surveillance process that could traumatize young girls across Washington," all "in the name of discriminating against trans people."[^5]

## What the complaint says the sex check would do

I-638 requires a student who competes in girls-only activities to provide a statement from her personal health care provider that "verifies the student's biological sex, relying only on one or more of the following: The student's reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or normal endogenously produced testosterone levels."[^3] The text names no method. The complaint argues that by naming these three bases the initiative "authorizes and invites inspection of the child's genitals, a genetic test, or a blood test for testosterone."[^1] It makes five more specific allegations:

- Transgender girls who have lived for years as girls would effectively be forced to disclose that they are transgender to classmates, coaches and school staff, and children with intersex traits may learn of those traits for the first time through a medically unnecessary test.[^1]
- A pediatrician's general knowledge of a longtime patient's sex "would not, by itself, establish verification based on the specific criteria required by the Initiative."[^1]
- The current medical eligibility form is valid for two years, so girls whose forms lack the new verification would need new documentation when the law takes effect, including mid-season for fall and winter sports.[^1]
- There is no blood test for "endogenous testosterone" levels, only for circulating levels, and a blood test is unlikely to be covered by health insurance. A genetic test typically needs a blood draw and an off-site lab, and results may take time to return. A cheek-swab version exists, but the complaint says limited availability and cost make it difficult to access.[^1]
- The initiative does not define a "normal" testosterone level or say which age groups the tests apply to.[^1]

These are the plaintiffs' allegations, to be tested in court. Our [November 6 report](/news/2026/11/06/i638-what-comes-next) covers what the text requires and who writes the rules.

## Let's Go Washington's answer, checked

Let's Go Washington, the conservative political organization led by hedge fund manager **Brian Heywood**, pushed I-638, and two other Let's Go Washington initiatives failed in the same election.[^2] Our [November 6 report](/news/2026/11/06/i638-what-comes-next) covers Heywood's role and what he said on election night. Let's Go Washington's executive director, **Darren Littell**, answered the lawsuit this way: "The ACLU, the Seattle School Board, Hate in Washington State and anyone else can raise lawsuits about protecting girls' sports all day, but their true colors are showing. They want boys in girls' sports at all costs, and they are willing to undermine democracy to get them there."[^2] The ACLU of Washington is counsel in this case. The Seattle school board voted Monday to authorize its own legal action and is not a party to this complaint.[^1][^7] Checked against the filing and the text:

**"Boys in girls' sports."** Trans girls are girls. Three of the four constitutional claims (privacy, equal rights and education) target a requirement that applies to every girl who competes and no boy.[^1][^3] The statement, as the Standard printed it, does not mention that requirement. **Tiffani Lennon**, executive director of Legal Voice, put the question he did not answer: "If you really want fairness in sports, why would you subject girls to sex verification? Why would you dissuade girls from participating in sports?"[^6]

**"Undermine democracy."** Seattle school board director **Joe Mizrahi**, a lead sponsor of the board's resolution to authorize legal action, said: "We can't pick one new law to follow and disregard other laws."[^7] He added: "When those laws potentially conflict, we actually have a system for resolving those conflicts, and that's called the courts."[^7] After the vote he told reporters: "In Washington state, we don't have a pre-initiative process to say if something is a valid law or not."[^7] The attorney general's office defends voter-approved initiatives in court, and its spokesperson, **Mike Faulk**, said: "When a new law is passed by the people or the Legislature, our role is to ensure it is defended regardless of our personal views about the policy."[^2] Asking a court whether a new law complies with the state constitution is the process the state already has for that question.

**"The vast majority of Washington State voters."** Littell said "the vast majority of Washington State voters agree that girls should have their sports and spaces protected."[^2] A vote for I-638 does not show what else voters agreed with, and the count does not support "vast" either. I-638 had about 56% on Tuesday morning with ballots still to count, and the Standard notes that left-leaning voters helped pass it.[^2][^7]

**"Expect the OSPI and WIAA to ensure..."** Littell said he expects "the OSPI and WIAA to ensure that all school districts protect fairness for girls' starting on December 3rd."[^2] Spokesperson **Hallie Herzberg** told KUOW that voters had made their opinion "loud and clear" and that Let's Go Washington expects OSPI and WIAA "to do everything in their power" to carry it out.[^7] The text puts the duty elsewhere. Section 3 requires "a school district board of directors or a voluntary nonprofit entity" to adopt the policies, and the amended RCW 28A.600.200 lets a board delegate athletics to WIAA.[^3] Section 4 keeps the superintendent of public instruction's duty to develop regulations and guidelines to eliminate sex discrimination in athletics, and now provides that athletic activities are offered to all students without regard to sex "except as provided in section 3 of this act."[^3] The text names no method, so the forms those bodies adopt will set it.

**The initiative's own premise.** I-638's findings say existing law requires a routine physical "which includes documentation of the student's sex assigned at birth," and that using "the existing physical examination process" ensures "equity, safety, and competitive balance."[^3] The complaint says the model medical eligibility form "does not require a medical provider to verify the sex of the student at all."[^1] We looked at WIAA's model medical eligibility form, which the complaint says is the only form submitted to the school.[^1] It has fields for name, date of birth, the provider's eligibility finding, the provider's contact details, allergies, medications and emergency contacts, and it says: "The Medical Eligibility Form is the only form that should be submitted to a school or sports organization."[^8] It has no field for sex. We did not review the separate history and exam forms, so we cannot say what records a provider keeps. WIAA says about 10 student-athletes statewide have disclosed being transgender, and it does not require disclosure, yet the verification requirement falls on every girl who competes.[^2]

## The attorney general was asked to act and has not said how the state will defend I-638

On November 6 the plaintiffs' lawyers wrote to Attorney General **Nick Brown** and Solicitor General **Noah Purcell**, asking the state to investigate and sue over I-638 and to respond by November 11. The suit was filed on November 9, two days before that date, and the complaint says the attorney general had not yet started an investigation or proceedings.[^1] The complaint also says that when WIAA recently considered amendments that would have barred transgender girls from girls' sports, the attorney general's Civil Rights Division "reviewed the amendments and concluded they would violate state law."[^1] The office now says it "will review litigation as it comes and discuss next steps," in a statement to the Washington State Standard.[^2] As of November 10, the sources we read do not say which of the four claims the state will contest.

## What happens before December 3, and who writes the rules

I-638 takes effect December 3, 30 days after the election.[^7] The Seattle school board voted unanimously Monday evening to give Superintendent Ben Shuldiner authority to take "legal action" against it. Board president Gina Topp and Mizrahi said the resolution does not specify the action or a timeline.[^7] Mizrahi said: "Right now, I don't see how we can implement the requirements of Initiative 638 without violating those other laws and constitutional protections."[^7] KUOW reports that WIAA would determine how to align school sports rules with the new law.[^7]

## What you can do

- **Ask Attorney General Nick Brown to answer in public.** Use the office's [contact page](https://www.atg.wa.gov/contactus.aspx) to ask how the state will answer the privacy and equal-rights claims, and whether it will tell the court if it agrees any claim has merit. The page notes that correspondence to the office is subject to public disclosure.[^9]
- **Go to your school board.** Ask it to say publicly what verification it will accept, to take the least invasive reading of the text and to protect students' medical records. Seattle's board has already authorized legal action.[^7]
- **Press WIAA and OSPI.** Let's Go Washington says it expects both to carry out I-638.[^2][^7] Ask each to say publicly what forms it will adopt, to accept records a girl's provider already holds, and to require nothing beyond the text.
- **Follow the case and back the people bringing it.** The ACLU of Washington lists the complaint and its press releases on its [case page](https://www.aclu-wa.org/cases/j-doe-a-v-washington-initiative-638-challenge/).[^10] Donate to or volunteer with TransFamilies, Legal Voice and the ACLU of Washington.
- **Tell your legislators** that I-638 must be amended as soon as the law allows. Our [November 6 report](/news/2026/11/06/i638-what-comes-next) explains the two-thirds vote that governs amendments in the first two years.

I-638 asks every girl who competes to prove her sex and removes trans girls from their teams. The plaintiffs are asking a court to hold it to four provisions already in the state constitution: privacy, equal rights, education without distinction on account of sex, and the rule against changing laws without printing them. Before December 3, the court, the attorney general, WIAA, OSPI and your school board will each make a choice that decides what I-638 means for a girl on a team. Tell them what you expect.
