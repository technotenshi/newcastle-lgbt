:Dateline Two ballot initiatives on Washington's November 3, 2026 ballot would strip a federal-style privacy protection for a student's private counseling notes and require a healthcare provider to verify a girl's sex before she can compete in school sports, according to a direct comparison of the initiatives' text against the state law they would repeal.[^1][^2][^3] Both measures, **IL26-001** and **IL26-638**, qualified for the ballot after signature drives funded almost entirely by **Let's Go Washington** (LGW), the political committee founded by hedge fund manager **Brian Heywood**.[^4][^5]

This is Let's Go Washington's fourth ballot cycle in three years. Heywood founded the Redmond-based committee in 2022[^6][^7] and has spent millions of his own money on its six 2024 initiatives,[^8] three of which the Legislature enacted outright while the other three went to voters.[^6] The state's campaign-finance watchdog has already fined the committee once,[^6] and as of August 2026 has an unresolved investigation open into whether it failed to disclose as much as $1.25 million in political advertising, which the committee denies.[^9][^10]

Lawmakers took the same path with IL26-001 and IL26-638: neither got a hearing. On January 9, 2026, **Senate Majority Leader Jamie Pedersen** and **House Speaker Laurie Jinkins**, both Democrats, told reporters they had no plans to hold hearings on either measure. "I'm certain that we will not be passing either of those initiatives," Pedersen said. "They'll be up to the voters to decide."[^11] When House Republican **Travis Couture** moved on January 28 to instruct the House Education Committee to hold hearings anyway, Democrats voted the motion down.[^12] Because lawmakers took no action, both measures went to the November ballot.[^13][^14]

Six months later, campaign finance filings show the "yes" side has outraised the combined opposition, which is anchored by the **ACLU of Washington**, the **Washington Education Association** (WEA), and **SEIU 775**, by roughly six to one.[^15]

## IL26-001 Would Reopen Access to Students' Private Counseling Notes

Supporters market IL26-001 as "Restoring Parents' Rights." Its official ballot summary is narrow and specific: it would re-enact **RCW 28A.605.005** exactly as it read when 2024's Parents' Bill of Rights initiative (I-2081) first passed, undoing every change the Legislature made through 2025's House Bill 1296.[^16] HB 1296's own text, which shows exactly what it struck and what it added, makes the comparison possible line by line.[^3]

The clearest change: HB 1296 added a standard, FERPA-style exemption that keeps a school employee's personal, unshared notes ("kept in the sole possession of the maker... not accessible or revealed to any other person") from counting as a disclosable "education record" at all. That's the provision that shields a counselor's private session notes from parents today. The original 2024 law had no such exemption, and went the opposite direction: it explicitly listed "records of any mental health counseling" and "records of any vocational counseling" among the records every enrolled student's parent has a statutory right to inspect. IL26-001 would restore both changes at once, removing the personal-notes exemption and reinstating the explicit right to inspect counseling records.[^1]

A second, narrower change involves the specific shield for parents accused of harming the child. Current law bars releasing a student's "health care, social work, counseling, or disciplinary" records, without a court order, to a parent who is the defendant in a criminal proceeding where the student is the named victim, or who is the target of a child-abuse or neglect investigation. IL26-001's restored text is narrower on both counts: it shields only "medical or health records or mental health counseling records," only "during the pendency of an investigation of child abuse or neglect" in which the parent is the target, and unless the parent has obtained a court order. It has no provision for a parent who is a criminal defendant, and it drops the child's social work and disciplinary records from the shield, so those records would be open to a parent under investigation.[^1]

The original text also tightens the clock for schools to produce requested student records, from current law's "reasonable period of time, but not more than 45 days" (matching the federal FERPA ceiling) to a flat 10 business days.[^16] That's a state deadline set below the federal maximum, not a violation of it: FERPA's 45-day window is a ceiling, not a floor, and federal regulation explicitly permits a state to require faster turnaround without risking federal education funding.

The attorney general's explanatory statement also says IL26-001 would remove from this statute the clause describing a right to a public education free of discrimination on grounds including sexual orientation, gender expression, and gender identity, and replace it with an assurance against discrimination based on the "sincerely held religious beliefs" of a child's family.[^16] The prohibitions in other chapters of state law are not repealed, but the statute's explicit protection for LGBTQ+ students would be gone.[^1]

Let's Go Washington disputes that the measure meaningfully affects private counseling conversations, arguing it governs records the school itself creates and maintains rather than the substance of what a student tells a counselor. The statute's own text cuts against that framing: the original language, which IL26-001 would restore, names "mental health counseling" records specifically and drops the exemption that currently keeps a counselor's unshared notes out of a parent's reach.[^1]

Advocates also argue that unfettered parental access to student records would lead to the outing of LGBTQ+ kids who look to teachers and counselors for support.[^17] That argument rests on the broader parental-access framework rather than on the counseling-records provision alone: the initiative's text does not require schools to notify parents of a student's sexual orientation or gender identity.[^1] The Washington State PTA cites data that 40% of youth experiencing homelessness identify as LGBTQIA2S+ and that 26% of them say they were forced out of their family home over their sexual orientation or gender identity,[^18] and the ACLU of Washington says IL26-001 would violate students' privacy.[^19] The Seattle Times editorial board, citing domestic violence experts, warns that the initiative could make it harder for a child to reach a safe adult,[^20] and more than 100 doctors and other medical professionals have signed a statement opposing the measures.[^21] The PTA and the editorial board both argue that reopening access to counseling records will deter students from confiding in a school counselor or nurse in the first place.[^18][^20]

## IL26-638 Would Create a Sex-Verification Requirement That Does Not Exist Today

Supporters market IL26-638 as "Fairness in Girls' Athletics." It would bar students the measure defines as "biologically male" from girls' school sports and require a healthcare provider's statement verifying sex based on reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or testosterone levels before any girl may compete.[^2][^22] A birth certificate alone would not satisfy the requirement.[^23]

The **Washington State PTA**'s formal analysis of the measure makes a concrete point often missing from the political debate: Washington's standard sports-physical form has no field for "sex" at all, and none of the three verification methods the initiative lists is part of a normal physical exam. Compliance would require something genuinely new for every girl seeking to play a school sport, not a checkbox added to an existing form.[^24]

The scale of that new requirement is disproportionate to the problem it claims to solve. The **Washington Interscholastic Activities Association** (WIAA) has said it is aware of about 10 transgender student athletes,[^23] out of more than 200,000 students who compete statewide, and the state superintendent has cited a comparable figure of five to ten.[^24] Opponents note that the state's sports policy has been in place since 2007, and supporters themselves say roughly 110,000 girls play middle and high school sports in the state.[^15] The measure applies only to girls; boys face no equivalent verification requirement,[^2][^23] which opponents argue may conflict with the Washington State Constitution's Equal Rights Amendment.[^21]

Whether the measure would, in practice, require a physical exam is genuinely disputed. LGW says providers would have discretion over the verification method and could use a student's existing medical history.[^7] The bill's text sets no floor requiring a physical exam,[^2] so "could require" is a more defensible characterization than "will require." But the clearest fact undercutting LGW's position comes independent of either campaign: the **American Academy of Pediatrics** asked to have its name removed from the "yes" campaign's official voters'-pamphlet statement, which had cited AAP guidelines as support for the measure.[^25][^21]

Family-medicine physician **Dr. Gina Martin**, quoted in a HeraldNet op-ed by retired school nurse **Katie Johnson**, said the measure "was clearly not written by somebody who practices medicine," since standard sports physicals focus on musculoskeletal and cardiac screening, not sex verification.[^26]

## A Six-to-One Money Gap

Campaign finance disclosures compiled by Ballotpedia show Let's Go Washington has raised roughly $4.45 million in support of IL26-638 alone, about $1.86 million of it from Heywood personally. On the opposition side, **No Hate in WA State** has raised approximately $447,000 and **Defend Washington** roughly $286,000. The top donors to the opposing committees are the Washington Education Association, the ACLU of Washington, SEIU 775, the Gender Justice League, and the SEIU Initiative Fund.[^15]

That funding gap comes on top of the more than 80 state organizations, including six Washington PFLAG chapters, that had already joined the opposition coalition by March. *(See our [earlier coverage](/news/2026/03/19/pflag-chapters-oppose-anti-lgbtq-initiatives) of that coalition's formation.)*

## What the Record Shows

Both measures follow the same shape: a narrow legal change marketed under a broad, sympathetic banner. IL26-001 does not ban school counseling or eliminate parental rights outright; it reopens one specific class of records, a child's mental-health counseling notes, to general parental inspection, removes the exemption that currently shields a counselor's unshared notes, and narrows, rather than eliminates, the separate shield that protects those same records from a parent under active abuse investigation. IL26-638 does not simply "protect girls' sports"; it creates a sex-verification requirement that does not exist in any form today, built around a population the state's own athletic association puts at about ten known athletes, out of more than 200,000 students who compete statewide.[^23][^24]

Neither conclusion requires overstating what either bill's text says. The clearest evidence against each initiative comes from checking its own words against the law it would replace, and from institutions with no stake in LGBTQ+ politics specifically: a domestic-violence coalition on IL26-001, a state PTA's reading of an actual sports-physical form on IL26-638, and the American Academy of Pediatrics distancing itself from the "yes" campaign's own voters'-pamphlet language.[^27][^24][^21] What both measures share is who is funding the case for them and who absorbs the cost if they pass: one donor's money against a coalition of medical, education, and civil-liberties organizations, and a narrow set of students, abuse survivors whose records become easier for an abusive parent to obtain, and transgender student-athletes, carrying a policy built for statewide political attention rather than a documented local problem.
