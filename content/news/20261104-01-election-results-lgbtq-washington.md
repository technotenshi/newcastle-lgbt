:Dateline Washington's first post-election returns bring two realities into view: Jaelynn Scott has a commanding lead in her state House race, while an initiative that would exclude transgender girls from girls' school sports is leading statewide.[^1][^2] LGBTQ Nation reports Scott's victory, and the Associated Press has called control of both chambers of Congress for Democrats.[^3][^4] None of those developments makes Washington's unfinished ballot count a certified result.

**Reporting snapshot: November 4, 2026, 1:28 p.m. Pacific Standard Time.** The statewide dashboard's latest update was 9:08 a.m.; King County's was November 3 at 8:12 p.m.; Pierce County's was November 4 at 9:06 a.m.[^2][^1][^5] National seat totals below come from AP's rendered dashboards checked around 1:21 p.m. Pacific.[^6][^7][^8] These sources update at different times. This article is a snapshot, not a live feed.

## Washington initiatives: a mixed result for student rights

These are statewide totals, not King County totals. Each row shows a **lead**, not an independently verified media call or certified outcome.[^2]

| Measure | Yes | No | Response leading by |
|---|---|---|---|
| IL26-638 | 1,119,192 (56.76%) | 852,539 (43.24%) | Yes, 266,653 votes[^2] |
| IL26-001 | 912,202 (47.25%) | 1,018,262 (52.75%) | No, 106,060 votes[^2] |
| IP26-645 | 830,106 (41.88%) | 1,152,229 (58.12%) | No, 322,123 votes[^2] |

### I-638: exclusion is leading, not yet law from this count

I-638 would generally prevent transgender girls from joining girls' interschool sports teams. Its text requires a student entering the specified female-only competitions to provide a statement from their personal health care provider verifying what it calls their "biological sex," based on reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or normal endogenous testosterone levels.[^9] The attorney general's explanation says this would affect public K–12 schools and private schools participating in WIAA or similar organizations.[^10]

The measure does not prescribe a genital examination for every girl. It does impose a medical sex-verification condition for the covered girls' competitions, without a corresponding requirement for boys' competitions.[^9] That is the concrete exclusion at stake, not an abstract disagreement over whether to support women's sports.

King County's count runs the other way: No has 297,246 votes, or 56.79%, against 226,142 Yes votes, or 43.21%.[^1] A county majority is not a statewide majority, and neither tally establishes why any individual voter voted as they did.

The Reign supporters' solidarity described in our earlier article remains meaningful. It also cannot substitute for the statewide count. The present lead is a reason to report the threat plainly and to keep supporting trans students, not to announce either a final defeat or an imagined reversal.

### I-001: No leads on the student-records rollback

Approval would restore the parents' rights statute to the version enacted through Initiative 2081, reversing later amendments.[^11] The initiative's strikeout text removes the statute's express statement about education free from discrimination on grounds including sexual orientation, gender expression and gender identity.[^12] It also changes access to school-held records and associated notice provisions.[^11]

That is not the same as repealing Washington's separate nondiscrimination laws. Those distinctions matter even when the outcome is politically welcome. A sustained No majority would reject this proposed rollback; the current lead alone does not settle it.

### I-645: No leads on the high-income tax repeal

I-645 would repeal the 9.9% tax on annual individual income over $1 million and prohibit specified state and local income taxes. The official ballot description says approval would decrease funding for public education and human services, primarily health care.[^2] Rejection would leave this repeal proposal unadopted. The No lead is larger than I-001's, and remains an unofficial count rather than a certified decision.

## Scott's reported victory, with history stated precisely

The official November count gives **Jaelynn Scott** 28,465 votes, or 87.21%, against **Evon McCorkle's** 3,899, or 11.95%, with 276 write-ins. Her lead is 24,566 votes.[^1] LGBTQ Nation reports that she has won and describes her as Washington's first trans legislator and the first out Black trans woman elected to serve in a U.S. state legislature.[^3] This is an attributed victory report; King County has not certified the result.

Scott leads the **Lavender Rights Project**, a Black trans feminist advocacy organization, and is a member of the Democratic Socialists of America.[^3] Our earlier profile explains the work and priorities behind the candidacy. Representation matters because a person arrives with experience, commitments and a constituency, not only a milestone.

The words **openly**, **woman** and **state legislature** matter. Rutgers records that Althea Garrison, a Black trans woman, was elected to the Massachusetts House in 1992 and was outed against her wishes after the election.[^13] Mo Turner, who is Black and nonbinary, previously served in Oklahoma's legislature.[^14] Andrea Jenkins became the first openly transgender African American woman elected to U.S. public office in 2017, at the Minneapolis City Council level.[^15] Scott's narrower distinction must not erase those predecessors.

Her LD37 race was not on Newcastle ballots. The local races covered in our guide were LD41 and WA-9; their current returns follow.

## Olympia: larger Democratic majorities if leads hold

Before the election, Democrats held 59 of 98 House seats and 30 of 49 Senate seats. All House seats and 24 Senate seats were contested.[^16] Aggregating the official House returns gives 68 Democratic leaders and 30 Republican leaders. This is an **if-leads-hold calculation**, not 68 declared Democratic winners.[^17]

| Chamber | Before election | If current leads hold | Conditional change |
|---|---|---|---|
| House, 98 seats | 59 D / 39 R | 68 D / 30 R; 69.39% D / 30.61% R | D +9 / R −9[^16][^17] |
| Senate, 49 seats | 30 D / 19 R | 31 D / 18 R; 63.27% D / 36.73% R | D +1 / R −1[^16][^18] |

The whole-Senate scenario is the Bellevue Chamber's published accounting, including seats not up for election; it is not a sum of only the 24 contests. The Chamber is a business advocacy organization, and its scenario is attributed here rather than adopted as a race call.[^18] A complete, independently verified inventory of **secured** post-election state legislative seats is not available for this draft.

A simple majority requires 50 House seats or 25 Senate seats. Two-thirds requires 66 or 33. The House scenario clears the two-thirds mark; the Senate scenario does not. Neither establishes a secured supermajority. The Standard identifies the 12th, 14th, 17th, 18th, 25th and 26th districts as places to watch for potential House pickups, and the 35th for a potential Senate pickup.[^16]

Across Washington's ten U.S. House contests, Democrats lead eight and Republicans two in the official export.[^17] WA-5 merits attention: Michael Baumgartner has 96,663 votes and Carmela Conroy 94,806, a 1,857-vote gap in this snapshot.[^2] That is not a ten-seat called delegation tally. Washington's U.S. Senate seats and statewide partisan executive offices were not on the 2026 ballot.[^19]

## Pierce County: sheriff appointment and oversight proposals trail

These county measures were not on Newcastle ballots. Their connection to our earlier Reign story is the sheriff's office, not a claim that a sports protest determined how voters voted.

| Measure | Approved response | Rejected response | Current lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amendment 53 | 77,192 (33.30%) | 154,639 (66.70%) | Rejected, 77,447 votes[^5] |
| Amendment 54 | 109,896 (47.66%) | 120,704 (52.34%) | Rejected, 10,808 votes[^5] |

**Amendment 53** would make the sheriff appointed by the county executive and confirmed by a Council majority. Approval would terminate the current elected term on January 1, 2027.[^23] With rejection leading, it would be wrong to announce that Sheriff Keith Swank has been removed. If rejection holds, this proposal would not end his term early.

**Amendment 54** would create a Public Safety Ombuds department for independent oversight of the sheriff and employees, subject to legal limits. Its duties would include publishing findings and recommendations and referring complaints to appropriate authorities; it would also establish a community advisory committee.[^24] If rejection holds, this new charter mechanism would not be created. That does not mean every other form of oversight disappears.

Our Reign report documents Swank's organizing for an anti-trans demonstration and the supporters' response. Residents' entitlement to fair treatment and accountable policing does not depend on this proposal passing.

## Nationally: AP calls Congress for Democrats

AP reports Democratic control of both the House and Senate.[^4] Its totals below distinguish called seats and continuing officeholders from uncalled races; they do not award seats to every current leader.

| Institution | Democratic / aligned | Republican | Uncalled |
|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. House, 435 seats | 237; 54.48% | 139; 31.95% | 59; 13.56%[^6] |
| U.S. Senate, 100 seats | 54 aligned; 54% | 44; 44% | 2; 2%[^7] |
| Governors, 50 states | 28; 56% | 20; 40% | 2; 4%[^8] |

The Senate's Democratic-aligned total includes the two continuing independents, **Angus King and Bernie Sanders**, who caucus with Democrats: 52 Democrats plus two independents, not 54 Democrats.[^25] Its starting balance was 53 Republicans to 47 Democratic-aligned members.[^7] The House threshold is 218; Democrats need 51 for Senate control with a Republican vice president.[^6][^7]

The governorship baseline was 24 Democratic and 26 Republican. NCSL reports Republican-to-Democratic flips in **Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas**.[^26] A final net change should wait for unresolved contests rather than treating a 28-seat secured tally as a complete 50-state result. AP's November 4 outstanding-races report identifies the Alaska and Mississippi Senate contests and the Alaska and Kansas governor contests as uncalled.[^27]

A change in Congress can change oversight and what legislation advances. It does not, by itself, repeal restrictions already in force or guarantee that trans people will be protected. The measure of the incoming majorities will be what they do with power, including whether they defend people targeted during the campaign.

## Representation milestones beyond Washington

This is a selected national overview, not an exhaustive list. **CNN projects wins** for the following candidates; AP reporting independently corroborates the distinctions for Pappas, Flanagan, Bottoms, Haaland and Donalds.[^28][^29] These are election outcomes attributed to news organizations, not a statement that the candidates have already taken their new offices.

| Candidate and party | New office | Precisely stated distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Chris Pappas (D) | New Hampshire, U.S. Senate | First openly gay man elected to the U.S. Senate.[^28][^29] |
| Peggy Flanagan (D) | Minnesota, U.S. Senate | First Native American woman elected to the U.S. Senate.[^28][^29] |
| Abdul El-Sayed (D) | Michigan, U.S. Senate | First Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate.[^28] |
| Keisha Lance Bottoms (D) | Georgia governor | First Black woman elected governor of a U.S. state.[^28][^29] |
| Deb Haaland (D) | New Mexico governor | First Native American woman elected governor of a U.S. state.[^28][^29] |
| Gina Hinojosa (D) | Texas governor | Texas' first Latina governor, according to AP; CNN describes the state's first Hispanic governor.[^30][^28] |
| Byron Donalds (R) | Florida governor | Florida's first Black governor and the nation's first Black Republican elected governor.[^28][^29] |

AP also reports that **Juliana Stratton's** Illinois Senate victory will pair her with **Tammy Duckworth**, making Illinois the first state represented by two women of color in the Senate. Duckworth is of Thai heritage.[^29] That is a milestone in the state's delegation, not a new 2026 election victory for Duckworth.

Historic representation deserves recognition across party lines. Identity is not a substitute for examining an official's record or holding them accountable to LGBTQ+ residents.

## What happens next

The official King and Pierce dashboards both list **November 4 at 4 p.m. Pacific** as their next expected update at this snapshot.[^1][^5] Washington's statewide page aggregates county reports; we have not verified a separate fixed time for its next statewide refresh.[^2] County canvassing boards certify on **November 24**, and the secretary of state's certification deadline is **December 3**.[^19] King County's results page separately schedules election-night precinct data for November 6, mid-election precinct data for November 12, and final precinct data for November 25, each by 4 p.m.[^31] Those data releases are not substitutes for the next count update.

No recount or runoff has been established for the local contests in this article. A narrow early margin is a reason to watch, not proof that a recount has been ordered. The next meaningful update is a newer official count or an attributable call, not speculation about how uncounted ballots must break.

Follow the [Washington statewide results](https://results.votewa.gov/results/public/washington/elections/20261103), [King County results](https://results.votewa.gov/results/public/king-county-wa/elections/20261103), and [Pierce County results](https://results.votewa.gov/results/public/pierce-county-wa/elections/20261103).

For Newcastle's LGBTQ+ community, the obligation after voting is the same as before it: defend students' access to school and sport, insist on fair public services, and hold elected officials to their commitments. A milestone in representation and a threat to trans students can exist in the same election. Reporting both honestly is more useful than declaring the entire night a victory or a defeat.
