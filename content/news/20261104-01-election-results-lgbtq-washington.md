### I-638: AP projects passage of the sports exclusion

I-638 would generally prevent transgender girls from joining girls' interschool sports teams. Its text requires a student entering the specified female-only competitions to provide a statement from their personal health care provider verifying what it calls their "biological sex," **based on reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or normal endogenous testosterone levels.**[^12] The attorney general's explanation says this would affect public K–12 schools and private schools participating in WIAA or similar organizations.[^13]

The measure does not prescribe a genital examination for every girl. It does impose a medical sex-verification condition for the covered girls' competitions, **without a corresponding requirement for boys' competitions.**[^12] That is the concrete exclusion at stake, not an abstract disagreement over whether to support women's sports.

King County's count runs the other way: **No** has 336,255 votes, or 57.25%, against 251,111 **Yes** votes, or 42.75%.[^2] A county majority is not a statewide majority, and neither tally establishes why any individual voter voted as they did.

The Reign supporters' solidarity described in our earlier article remains meaningful. Supporting trans students now means preparing for the projected exclusion while continuing to defend their access to sport.

### I-001: No leads on the student-records rollback

Approval would restore the parents' rights statute to the version enacted through Initiative 2081, reversing later amendments.[^14] The initiative's strikeout text removes the statute's express statement about education free from discrimination on grounds including sexual orientation, gender expression and gender identity.[^15] It also changes access to school-held records and associated notice provisions.[^14]

That is not the same as repealing Washington's separate nondiscrimination laws. Those distinctions matter even when the outcome is politically welcome. A sustained No majority would reject this proposed rollback; the current lead alone does not settle it.

### I-645: No leads on the high-income tax repeal

I-645 would repeal the 9.9% tax on annual individual income over $1 million and prohibit specified state and local income taxes. The tax applies beginning with **2028 income**; married couples and registered domestic partners share a single **$1 million standard deduction**. Repeal would eliminate future revenue for public education, health care and other human services, as well as child care and early learning.[^16] Our [earlier election article](/news/2026/10/28/washington-election-transgender-rights) endorsed No in support of public services LGBTQ+ people rely on. Rejection would leave this repeal proposal unadopted. The **No** lead is larger than I-001's, and remains an unofficial count rather than a certified decision.

## I-638: the threat to students and the work ahead

For trans girls, the stakes are a place on the team alongside their peers. A vote cannot make those children less deserving of friendship, belonging or the chance to play.

### What advocates are saying

Before the election, **ACLU of Washington** warned that the damage goes beyond eligibility. In an October 2 commentary, its media and communications strategist **LaVendrick Smith** wrote that sports bans on transgender girls "send the hateful message that some children don't belong simply because of who they are."[^17] That is the ACLU's pre-election assessment, not a new statement responding to Wednesday's count.

The **No Hate in WA State** coalition did respond to the first returns on November 3. Campaign manager **Libby Watson** expressed hope that later ballots would reject I-638. That election-night hope predates AP's call and is not evidence that a reversal is likely. In the same release, **Adrianna Suluai**, UTOPIA Washington's policy and advocacy director and a coalition executive-board member, promised continued support regardless of the outcome:[^18]

> "To all the trans kids, to all of the girls playing elementary, middle and high school sports, we care about you and there are people who will never stop fighting for you."
> *Adrianna Suluai, in No Hate in WA State's November 3 response*[^18]

### After AP's call, what comes next?

The state's initiative-specific fiscal analysis identifies **December 3, 2026** as the effective date if voters approve I-638 and says its provisions apply prospectively, not retroactively. It anticipates the Office of the Superintendent of Public Instruction updating guidance and school districts revising policies and procedures.[^19] That analysis describes expected implementation work; it is not a set of instructions already issued to schools.

**As of the November 4 reporting for this section, the first-party materials reviewed did not establish an announced I-638 lawsuit, an injunction blocking it, or post-election implementation guidance from OSPI or WIAA.** Legal opposition is not the same as a filed case, and a filed case would not itself guarantee that enforcement is delayed. Families should not be told to count on a court rescue that has not been established.

There are existing places to seek help. The Washington State LGBTQ Commission's sports resource page directs families to school civil-rights coordinators, OSPI's discrimination-complaint resources and the **Office of the Education Ombuds**.[^20] These are assistance routes, not promises of continued eligibility under a changed law. Supporting students means helping them seek answers about their school's policies, addressing harassment and exclusion, and maintaining their place in community even as the legal rules change. Neither the unfinished count nor AP's projected passage is a reason to leave trans children to face this alone.

## Scott's reported victory, with history stated precisely

The official November count gives **Jaelynn Scott** 32,069 votes, or 87.32%, against **Evon McCorkle's** 4,344, or 11.83%, with 311 write-ins. Her lead is 27,725 votes.[^2] LGBTQ Nation reports that she has won and describes her as Washington's first trans legislator and the first out Black trans woman elected to serve in a U.S. state legislature.[^3] This is an attributed victory report; King County has not certified the result.

Scott leads the **Lavender Rights Project**, a Black trans feminist advocacy organization, and is a member of the Democratic Socialists of America.[^3] Our earlier profile explains the work and priorities behind the candidacy. Representation matters because a person arrives with experience, commitments and a constituency, not only a milestone.

The words **openly**, **woman** and **state legislature** matter. Rutgers records that Althea Garrison, a Black trans woman, was elected to the Massachusetts House in 1992 and was outed against her wishes after the election.[^21] Mo Turner, who is Black and nonbinary, previously served in Oklahoma's legislature.[^22] Andrea Jenkins became the first openly transgender African American woman elected to U.S. public office in 2017, at the Minneapolis City Council level.[^23] Scott's narrower distinction must not erase those predecessors.

Her LD37 race was not on Newcastle ballots. The local races covered in our guide were LD41 and WA-9; their current returns follow.

**Amendment 53** would make the sheriff appointed by the county executive and confirmed by a Council majority. Approval would terminate the current elected term on January 1, 2027.[^31] With rejection leading, it would be wrong to announce that Sheriff Keith Swank has been removed. If rejection holds, this proposal would not end his term early.

Our Reign report documents Swank's organizing for an anti-trans demonstration and the supporters' response. Residents' entitlement to fair treatment and accountable policing does not depend on this proposal passing.

The measure of the incoming majorities will be what they do with power, including whether they defend people targeted during the campaign.

## Representation milestones beyond Washington

This is a selected national overview, not an exhaustive list. **CNN projects wins** for the following candidates; AP reporting independently corroborates the distinctions for Pappas, Flanagan, Bottoms, Haaland and Donalds.[^36][^37] These are election outcomes attributed to news organizations, not a statement that the candidates have already taken their new offices.

| Candidate and party | New office | Precisely stated distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Chris Pappas (D) | New Hampshire, U.S. Senate | First openly gay man elected to the U.S. Senate.[^36][^37] |
| Peggy Flanagan (D) | Minnesota, U.S. Senate | First Native American woman elected to the U.S. Senate.[^36][^37] |
| Abdul El-Sayed (D) | Michigan, U.S. Senate | First Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate.[^36] |
| Keisha Lance Bottoms (D) | Georgia governor | First Black woman elected governor of a U.S. state.[^36][^37] |
| Deb Haaland (D) | New Mexico governor | First Native American woman elected governor of a U.S. state.[^36][^37] |
| Gina Hinojosa (D) | Texas governor | Texas' first Latina governor, according to AP; CNN describes the state's first Hispanic governor.[^38][^36] |
| Byron Donalds (R) | Florida governor | Florida's first Black governor and the nation's first Black Republican elected governor.[^36][^37] |

AP also reports that **Juliana Stratton's** Illinois Senate victory will pair her with **Tammy Duckworth**, making Illinois the first state represented by two women of color in the Senate. Duckworth is of Thai heritage.[^37] That is a milestone in the state's delegation, not a new 2026 election victory for Duckworth.

Historic representation deserves recognition across party lines. Identity is not a substitute for examining an official's record or holding them accountable to LGBTQ+ residents.
